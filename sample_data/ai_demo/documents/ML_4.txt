In machine learning, a computer program improves its task performance by learning regularities from examples. Rather than specifying every decision as a fixed rule, developers train a model on data so it can respond to previously unseen cases. Results are strongly affected by whether the examples are relevant, sufficient, and trustworthy.

Learning with labelled examples is called supervised learning. It includes classification, which chooses a category such as spam or not spam, and regression, which estimates a quantity such as a property's value. Unsupervised methods work without target answers; clustering can reveal groups within the observations.

A normal workflow gathers and prepares data, separates examples for training and testing, chooses a method, fits its parameters, and measures performance. For uneven class distributions, accuracy can hide poor performance. Precision, recall, F1, and confusion-matrix values give a broader view.

These methods support medical tools, image analysis, recommendations, language applications, and equipment maintenance. A model may overfit when it learns details specific to its training set instead of general patterns. Regularisation, cross-validation, careful feature selection, and evaluation on untouched examples help estimate its ability to generalise.
